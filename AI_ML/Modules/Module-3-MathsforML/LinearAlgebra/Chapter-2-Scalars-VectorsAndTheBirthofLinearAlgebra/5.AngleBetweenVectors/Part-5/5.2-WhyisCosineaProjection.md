# Part 5 — Geometry

# 5.2 — Why is Cosine a Projection?

---

# 🎯 Learning Objective

By the end of this section, you will understand:

* What a **projection** actually is.
* Why cosine naturally appears in the dot product.
* Why the dot product measures **alignment**, not just multiplication.
* Why cosine similarity works in Machine Learning.

This is one of the most important geometric ideas in Linear Algebra.

---

# A Different Way to Look at Two Vectors

Suppose we have two vectors.

```math
\mathbf{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{y}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
y_1\\
y_2
\end{bmatrix}
```

Both start from the same origin.

Instead of asking

> **"What is the angle?"**

let's ask a different question.

> **"How much of vector y lies in the direction of vector x?"**

That question leads to the idea of **projection**.

---

# Imagine a Flashlight

Suppose sunlight is coming vertically downward.

Imagine vector **y** casting a shadow onto vector **x**.

```text
                 y
                /
               /
              /
             /
            ●
           /|
          / |
         /  |
        /θ  |
O────────────────────► x

          Shadow
```

The shadow of **y** on **x** is called the **projection** of **y** onto **x**.

Notice something interesting.

We are **not changing the direction of x**.

We are only asking

> **How much of y points along x?**

---

# When Are Two Vectors Most Similar?

Imagine gradually rotating vector **y**.

### Case 1 — Same Direction

```text
────────►
────────►
```

The entire vector points in the same direction.

Projection = Full length.

---

### Case 2 — Slightly Rotated

```text
────────►

      ↗
```

Some of the vector still points along x.

Projection becomes smaller.

---

### Case 3 — Perpendicular

```text
────────►

      ↑
```

Nothing points along x.

Projection becomes zero.

---

### Case 4 — Opposite Direction

```text
────────►

◄────────
```

Now the projection points backwards.

It becomes negative.

---

# Where Does Cosine Come From?

Remember the right triangle from trigonometry.

```text
                 y
                ●
               /|
              / |
             /  |
            /θ  |
           /    |
          ●-----●
          Projection
```

From basic trigonometry,

```text
cosθ
=
Adjacent
──────────
Hypotenuse
```

Here,

* Hypotenuse = length of vector y
* Adjacent = projection of y onto x

Therefore,

```math
\boxed{
\text{Projection of }\mathbf{y}\text{ onto }\mathbf{x}
=
\|\mathbf{y}\|\cos\theta
}
```

This is the **first appearance of cosine**.

Notice that cosine is **not** measuring similarity directly.

It is measuring **how much of one vector points in another direction**.

---

# A Beautiful Interpretation

Suppose

```text
Length of y = 10
```

---

### If θ = 0°

```text
cos0° = 1
```

Projection

```text
10 × 1 = 10
```

Entire vector contributes.

---

### If θ = 60°

```text
cos60° = 0.5
```

Projection

```text
10 × 0.5 = 5
```

Only half contributes.

---

### If θ = 90°

```text
cos90° = 0
```

Projection

```text
10 × 0 = 0
```

Nothing contributes.

---

### If θ = 180°

```text
cos180° = -1
```

Projection

```text
10 × (-1)
=
-10
```

Entire contribution is in the opposite direction.

---

# Now the Dot Product Makes Sense

Earlier we memorized

```math
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
=
\|\mathbf{x}\|
\|\mathbf{y}\|
\cos\theta
```

Now let's understand it.

We already know

```math
\|\mathbf{y}\|\cos\theta
```

is the **projection** of **y** onto **x**.

Multiply this projection by

```math
\|\mathbf{x}\|
```

and we obtain

```math
\boxed{
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
=
\|\mathbf{x}\|
\times
(\text{Projection of }\mathbf{y}\text{ onto }\mathbf{x})
}
```

This is one of the deepest interpretations of the dot product.

The dot product is **not just multiplication**.

It is:

> **The length of one vector multiplied by how much of the other vector points in its direction.**

---

# Why Does This Measure Similarity?

Suppose two sentence embeddings point in almost the same direction.

Their projection onto each other is almost the full vector.

Therefore,

* cosine is close to **1**
* dot product is large.

If the embeddings are unrelated,

their projection is tiny.

Therefore,

* cosine is close to **0**
* dot product becomes small.

If they represent opposite meanings,

the projection is negative.

Therefore,

* cosine becomes negative.
* dot product also becomes negative.

This is why cosine similarity is so powerful in Machine Learning.

It compares **directional alignment**, not just numerical values.

---

# 🤖 Machine Learning Lens

Imagine two document embeddings.

### Document A

> "Cats are wonderful pets."

### Document B

> "Cats make great companions."

The two vectors point in almost the same direction.

Large projection.

Large cosine.

Large similarity.

---

Now consider

### Document C

> "I strongly dislike cats."

Its embedding points in almost the opposite direction.

Projection becomes negative.

Cosine becomes negative.

The model immediately understands that the semantic meaning is opposite.

---

# 💡 Think Like a Mathematician

Earlier, cosine looked like a mysterious trigonometric function.

Now we can reinterpret it completely.

> **Cosine is the fraction of one vector that points in the direction of another vector.**

And the dot product simply scales that fraction by the length of the reference vector.

Once you see the dot product as **projection**, almost every concept in Linear Algebra—including orthogonality, least squares, PCA, projections onto subspaces, and even attention mechanisms in Transformers—becomes much more intuitive.

---

# 📦 Key Takeaways

* **Projection** measures how much of one vector lies in the direction of another.
* The projection of **y** onto **x** is:

```math
\boxed{
\|\mathbf{y}\|\cos\theta
}
```

* The dot product can be interpreted as:

```math
\boxed{
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
=
\|\mathbf{x}\|
\times
(\text{Projection of }\mathbf{y}\text{ onto }\mathbf{x})
}
```

* Cosine is not an arbitrary function in the dot product—it appears naturally because **projection is governed by cosine**.
* This geometric interpretation is the foundation of **cosine similarity**, one of the most widely used similarity measures in Machine Learning.
