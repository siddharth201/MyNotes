## What is a Hyperplane?
In machine learning and geometry, a hyperplane is a flat decision boundary that divides a multidimensional space into distinct parts.
Think of it as a geometric divider. In a machine learning algorithm like a Support Vector Machine (SVM), the hyperplane acts as the "line in the sand" that separates different classes of data (for example, separating spam emails from regular emails).

## Space vs. Hyperplane: Understanding the Dimensions
The defining rule of a hyperplane is that its dimension is always exactly one less than the dimension of the space surrounding it (ambient space). If your space has $N$ dimensions, the hyperplane has $N-1$ dimensions.
Here is how a hyperplane changes as you move through different dimensions:

| Dimensions of the Space | What the Space Looks Like | Dimension of the Hyperplane ($N-1$) | What the Hyperplane Looks Like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1D Space | A single line (X-axis) | 0D | A Point (splits the line into left and right) |
| 2D Space | A flat surface (X and Y axes) | 1D | A Line (splits the surface into two sides) |
| 3D Space | A physical room (X, Y, and Z axes) | 2D | A Flat Sheet/Wall (splits the room into two halves) |
| 4D+ Space | High-dimensional data space | 3D or higher | A Hyperplane (too complex to visualize, but mathematically splits the space) |


## Why do we need the concept of a "Hyperplane"?
When humans look at data on a standard chart (2D), we use a line to separate groups. When we look at a 3D model, we use a flat plane to separate groups.
However, machine learning data often contains dozens, hundreds, or thousands of features (dimensions). Since we cannot easily name a 100-dimensional separating wall, mathematicians use the universal term hyperplane to describe a flat separator in any dimensional space.
## How it works in Supervised Learning
Imagine you are classifying houses as "Expensive" or "Affordable":

* If you only look at size (1D space), the hyperplane is a single price point (e.g., 2,000 sq ft).
* If you look at size and location score (2D space), the hyperplane is a straight line drawn on a graph.
* If you look at size, location score, and age of the house (3D space), the hyperplane becomes a flat 2D sheet slicing through the data points.

## Q. A line in 2d space can be called as Hyperplane?

Yes, a line in 2D space is considered a hyperplane.
In mathematics and machine learning, a hyperplane is defined as a subspace whose dimension is exactly one less than its ambient space ($n - 1$, where $n$ is the dimension of the space).
Here is how hyperplanes scale across different dimensions:

| Ambient Space | Dimensions ($n$) | Hyperplane ($n - 1$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1D Space (Line) | $1$ | A Point (0D) |
| 2D Space (Plane) | $2$ | A Line (1D) |
| 3D Space (Room) | $3$ | A Flat Plane (2D) |
| 4D Space and above | $n$ | An $n-1$ Hyperplane |

### Why it matters
In classification algorithms like Support Vector Machines (SVMs), a hyperplane is used as a decision boundary to separate data classes.

* In a 2D dataset (e.g., plotting weight vs. height), the "hyperplane" that separates your two groups is simply a straight line.
