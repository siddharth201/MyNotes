## What is Euler’s number \(e\)?

Euler’s number, written as \(e\), is an important mathematical constant:

\[
e \approx 2.718281828\ldots
\]

Like \(\pi\), its decimal digits continue forever without repeating.

It is the **base of natural logarithms**. The function \(e^x\) is special because its rate of change is equal to its value:

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^x\right)=e^x
\]

The number \(e\) can also be defined by this limit:

\[
e=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n
\]

It appears often in:

- continuous growth and decay,
- compound interest,
- population models,
- radioactive decay,
- calculus,
- probability.

## What is \(e^{-\infty}\)?

Strictly speaking, \(\infty\) is not an ordinary number. The expression \(e^{-\infty}\) means:

\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{-x}
\]

Since

\[
e^{-x}=\frac{1}{e^x}
\]

and \(e^x\) becomes larger and larger as \(x\) increases, the fraction becomes smaller and smaller:

\[
\frac{1}{e^x}\to 0
\]

Therefore,

\[
\boxed{e^{-\infty}=0}
\]

More accurately,

\[
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{-x}=0}
\]

The value gets **closer and closer to \(0\)** but never actually reaches \(0\) for any finite \(x\).
